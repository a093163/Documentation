## Leadership Summary: How the Benchmark Works

We are building a benchmark for how long tasks typically take, while also protecting that benchmark from bad timing records.

A common issue in task systems is that someone may finish the work but forget to close the task. When that happens, the system may show a task as taking **36 hours** when the real work was closer to **2 hours**. If we leave those records in, the benchmark becomes inflated and less fair.

### What we do
We use a statistical method to identify only the **extreme high task times** that are far outside the normal pattern for that type of work. We do this **within each task group**, so we are always comparing similar work to similar work.

After those likely timing errors are removed, we calculate benchmark statistics that help leaders understand:

- what a **typical** task looks like
- what the **overall average** looks like
- how much the results are being influenced by a smaller number of longer tasks

---

# Simple analogy
Think of tracking how long a meeting lasts.

Most meetings may run about an hour. But once in a while, someone forgets to stop the timer and the meeting appears to last all night.

If we simply average all those times, we would end up saying:

> “Our meetings usually last much longer than they really do.”

So instead, we first remove the records that are most likely due to the timer being left on. Then we look at the cleaned results to understand what is normal.

---

# What the benchmark tells leaders

## 1) Median = typical task time
The **median** is the middle value when all task times are lined up from shortest to longest.

In plain terms:

> The median tells us what a typical task looks like.

This is often the best coaching benchmark because it is less affected by a small number of unusually long tasks.

---

## 2) Average = overall pull of the workload
The **average** still matters, but it answers a slightly different question.

In plain terms:

> The average shows the overall time level, including the effect of longer tasks.

So if the average is much higher than the median, that tells us there is a longer tail of tasks pulling the average upward.

---

## 3) Skew = how much longer tasks are pulling the average away from typical
We now also show a measure of **skew**.

In plain language:

> Skew tells us whether the average is close to the typical task time, or whether it is being pulled upward by a smaller number of long tasks.

This is important because it helps leaders know whether the **average** is a good summary of normal work.

---

# How to interpret skew

## Roughly symmetric
- Average and median are close
- Either one gives a similar picture
- The work is fairly balanced

### Leadership takeaway
The average is a reasonable summary of typical work.

---

## Slight skew
- There are some longer tasks
- The average is a little higher than the median
- Typical work is still fairly stable

### Leadership takeaway
The average is still usable, but the median is a slightly better reflection of normal work.

---

## Moderate skew
- Longer tasks are noticeably pulling up the average
- The average is no longer the best description of a typical task

### Leadership takeaway
Use the **median as the primary benchmark** for typical handling time.  
Use the **average as supporting context**.

---

## Strong skew
- A relatively small number of long tasks are strongly influencing the average
- The average no longer represents the typical task very well

### Leadership takeaway
Focus on the **median** for coaching.  
Use the average only to understand the impact of the longer tail.

---

# Why this matters for coaching
This benchmark is intended to help leaders identify coaching or process opportunities — not to manage individual performance.

That means the benchmark should be:

- fair
- stable
- easy to interpret
- not overly influenced by bad data or rare extreme cases

By using both:
- a cleaned benchmark
- and a skew indicator

leaders can better tell the difference between:

### Situation A
Most tasks are genuinely taking longer than expected

### Situation B
Most tasks are normal, but a smaller number of much longer tasks are pulling the average up

Those lead to very different coaching conversations.

---

# What leaders should focus on
When reviewing the output, leaders should think about it this way:

- **Median** = typical task handling time
- **Average** = overall level including longer tasks
- **Skew** = how much the average is being pulled away from typical
- **Outlier rate** = how much likely bad timing data had to be removed

---

# Recommended interpretation
## If skew is low
The average is a reasonable summary.

## If skew is moderate or strong
The median should be used as the main benchmark for coaching.

That is the simplest rule of thumb.

# Slide-friendly version

## How the benchmark is built
- We compare each task only to similar tasks
- We identify and remove only the extreme high times that are most likely timing errors
- We then calculate a cleaned benchmark for that task group

## What the numbers mean
- **Median** = typical task time
- **Average** = overall time level
- **Skew** = whether longer tasks are pulling the average up

## How to use it
- Low skew: average and median tell a similar story
- Moderate/high skew: median is the better coaching benchmark

---

# Short talk track
Here’s a version you could say out loud:

> We know some task times are overstated because tasks are occasionally left open in the system after the work is finished. To prevent those records from distorting the benchmark, we statistically remove only the extreme high values that fall far outside the normal pattern for similar work. We then look at both the median and the average. The median tells us the typical task time, while the average shows the overall impact of longer tasks. We also measure skew, which tells us whether the average is being pulled away from what is typical. If skew is moderate or high, we rely more on the median for coaching conversations.
